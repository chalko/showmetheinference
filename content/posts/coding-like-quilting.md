There was a time when, if your grandmother gave you a handmade quilt, it was a
valuable piece of property. You took care of it and maybe even treasured it. A
quilter's skill was valued by the community.

Then Walmart started selling blankets for $20. They're not as nice as what
Grandma made, but they're $20. You can buy a bunch of blankets, and you throw
them away when they get threadbare or stained.

I could lament the fact that I used to write beautiful code—that my software was
a piece of art meant to be maintained for years or decades. I could lament, but
that's not my point, and that's not the problem I think tech is facing. I'm over
the fact that my ability to write beautiful code is about as valuable as the
ability to make a beautiful quilt. That is what machines do. That is what AI
does.

The problem I think we're really facing is... well, I'll tell a story.

My youngest son was getting married, and we rented a large VRBO house for all of
my family—seven kids, spouses, and grandchildren. That was a lot of people, and
the house, while large and well-appointed, just didn't have enough blankets.

So, I went to Walmart and bought three $20 blankets.

After the wedding, which was beautiful, it was time to go home. We packed up
those blankets and brought them home. The problem was, our linen closet was
full. I already had all the blankets I needed. I didn't have a spot to store
those extra blankets. Really, I probably should have just thrown them away, but
they were good blankets and I had just bought them.

So... my wife looked at the linen closet and asked if we should throw away
the quilt her grandmother made her.

---

And that is the problem we're facing with AI coding.

It's practically free to generate, so the impulse is, _"Why not? Have it build
that feature."_ But cheap creation ignores the hidden tax: every line of code
must be stored, audited, and maintained. We haven't learned how to manage
hundreds of bespoke programs, or how to triage what AI code is actually worth
keeping.

I think our industry is going to have to get used to viewing code as
disposable—like a $20 blanket.

In my own work with local AI, that means exploring a few practical questions:

- How do we design a staff of agents that actively decides what code to keep and
  what to throw away?
- How do we protect what actually has value—like tests and APIs—while letting
  the implementation be completely disposable?

I'll let you know what I learn along the way.
